              A clerk's hint

              People write left to right, and the route turns round at the top and bottom of the rack. So where it turns, two words that belong side by side sit next to each other on the tape. Find a pair that makes sense together, then count the words between pairs: that's how many rows the rack had.

              Ask the night clerk

              Stuck? After hours, the night clerk tries every route through the rack, thousands of them, in about a second. The clerk can't read, though. Tell it a few pairs of words you're sure sit side by side, and it throws out every route that pulls them apart.

                    How the office works

                    This is a route cipher. It doesn't change any words, it only shuffles them. The message goes into the rack left to right, a row at a time. Then it comes out up and down the columns, in an order called the key: -1 2 -3 4 means up the first column, down the second, up the third, down the fourth.

                    Shuffling alone is easy to undo, so the clerks swap the important words for code words (the money goes out as DONKEY) and fill any empty pigeonholes with harmless words. Untangle the route and you still read "meet GULL by BANDSTAND".

                    A challenge link carries the scrambled words and a seal: a fingerprint of the right order that can tell when you've got it, but can't be turned back into the message. The route itself never leaves your screen. A new telegram reaches the counter every day, the same one for everybody, picked by the date.

                    The night clerk is a brute-force attack: try every possible key. A computer is happy to try thousands, but it can't tell a sentence from a jumble, so your hunches do the reading. With no hunches at all, the clerk can still break a challenge link's seal, by trying routes until one matches its fingerprint. That's why real secrets use keys with far more possibilities than a pigeonhole rack.

                    The rail fence is a transposition too, letter by letter. It's quick to do with a pencil, and quick to crack: there are only a handful of rail counts to try, and a dictionary spots the one that reads. Its letters are still English letters, just in a different order, which is how the clerk tells it from a cipher that swaps them.

                    Telegraph clerks liked it because every word is a real word. Strings of random letters get mangled on a busy wire; whole words come through. The page is built the same way: you can only pick rack sizes that fit and routes that exist, so there's no wrong button to press. All the shuffling is Python, running right here in your browser.
