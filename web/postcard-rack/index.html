    How a note hides on a postcard

    Hiding a secret so well that nobody suspects there is one is called steganography, from the Greek for "covered writing". The postcards here use a null cipher: the hidden note isn't scrambled at all. Its letters sit inside an ordinary message at places agreed in advance, and every other letter is padding.

    The famous one is a letter smuggled to a Royalist prisoner in 1642, during the English Civil War. His jailers read it and found nothing odd. Counting three letters on from every punctuation mark spelled out where the secret way out of the chapel was, and by the next morning he was gone.

    The punctuation rule has small print, and it matters. Every mark starts the count again, so a second mark before you get there restarts it. Marks never count as letters. Spaces don't count at all. An extra comma in the wrong place, and the note falls apart.

    Writing one is much harder than finding one: every sentence has to make sense and put the right letter in the right place. A list gets round that, because nobody expects a list to make sense. That's why the writing desk uses Nan's Scrabble words: any word can be a good Scrabble word, so any word can carry a letter.

    The rack's cards use a few rules from the same era: letters after punctuation, the first letter of every word or of every capital word, and the 2nd letter of every 2nd word (or the 3rd of every 3rd). Hold on to that last one: one card uses it.

    On a card from a friend or your own words, "Reveal" does what a suspicious jailer could have done: try every rule and every count, and keep the one that reads as real words. A dictionary splits the letters back into words, and a reading only counts if it's real words all the way through, and not just a lucky run of "a", "at" and "me".
